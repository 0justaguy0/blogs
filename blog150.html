Vamadeva's Solar Self-Absorption Rite

There is a client who comes to a jyotiṣi with a complaint that has no name in the standard remedial vocabulary.

Nothing is broken. The marriage exists. The income exists. The health is unremarkable. And yet this person has spent thirty years being passed over — the promotion that went to a less capable colleague, the book that was rejected by eleven publishers and then became a bestseller for someone else, the family in which their opinion is heard and then quietly not followed, the room in which they speak and the conversation resumes as though a door had closed.

They have worn the ruby. They have done the Sūrya-arghya every Sunday for nine years. They have given wheat and jaggery and copper, they have recited the Āditya Hṛdayam eleven thousand times, and they will tell you — politely, because they are that kind of person — that it has not changed the fundamental fact of being unseen.

This is not a weak Sun in the ordinary sense. A weak Sun gives low confidence, a difficult father, poor authority. This is something more specific: a chart in which the Ātma-kāraka is present but not visible — where the native's essence is intact and their recognition is not. The tradition has an exact name for the gap between what a person is and what the world is willing to concede that they are, and it has a rishi who closed that gap in the most extreme way available to a human being.

He closed it in the womb.

His name is Vamadeva (वामदेव) — "he whose deity is vāma", the beautiful one, the one on the left.

And the technique he is remembered for is not a propitiation of the Sun. It is an identification with it — a rite in which the native does not ask the Sun for anything, because the Sun is the one graha in the sky that cannot be given a gift.

Part One: The Rishi the Sources Actually Describe

1. A poet of the fourth book, and a priest of a king

Vamadeva is not a Purāṇic figure whose biography was invented late. He is one of the ten family-book seers of the Ṛgveda: the whole of Maṇḍala 4 — one hundred and fifty-eight hymns — is attributed to him and to his line.

The sources give him a plain, almost bureaucratic identity:

Vamadeva is a rishi in Hindu literature. He is credited as the author of Maṇḍala 4 of the Ṛgveda […] He is described to be the son of a sage named Gotama and the brother of Nodhasa, who is also associated with hymns in the Ṛgveda. Vamadeva is the father of Bṛhaduktha, and belongs to the lineage of Sage Aṅgiras.

So: son of Gautama, brother of Nodhas, father of Bṛhaduktha, of the Aṅgirasa line. The Aṅgirasa lineage matters more than it looks. This is the family of fire — of Agni, of the aṅgāra, the live coal. Vamadeva belongs to the one Vedic clan whose entire inheritance is the management of heat, and specifically of heat that is carried rather than burned.

He is also, in the Rāmāyaṇa, one of the priests of Daśaratha in the Bāla-kāṇḍa — the officiating circle around the king whose whole problem was that he could not produce an heir, and whose remedy was a fire-ritual that produced four sons out of a vessel of payasam. Keep that detail. Vamadeva was standing in the room where the tradition's most famous fertility rite was performed, and his own story begins with the opposite situation: not a man who cannot produce a child, but a child that arrives already complete.

2. The sentence he spoke from inside his mother

Here is the passage that makes Vamadeva unique among the ṛṣis, and it is preserved in three separate places — the Aitareya Upaniṣad, the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad, and the Ṛgveda itself.

The Aitareya, in its second chapter, describes the self-knowledge of the one who realises the Ātman, and then names its example. Vamadeva, it says, knew this while he was still in the womb — and he spoke, from within his mother, the words that later became a Vedic verse. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka quotes the same declaration to prove the same point: that when a man knows the Self, he does not merely think he is Brahman, he becomes the totality of what is.

And the verse itself is Ṛgveda 4.26.1 — his own hymn — and it is one of the most audacious lines in the entire corpus:

aham manur abhavaṃ sūryaś cāham

"I was Manu. I became Sūrya."

Read the tense carefully, because the whole rite turns on it, and almost every popular retelling gets it wrong.

He does not say I am the Sun. He does not say I worship the Sun. He says I became — abhāvam, the aorist, the tense of a completed event, a thing that has already happened and cannot be undone. The identification is not a present-tense claim of status. It is a report of a change of state that has already occurred.

And the sentence has a third clause, which the popular tradition usually drops and which is operationally the most important part:

…aham kakuth-salaḥ — "…I am Kakuthsala."

Kakuthsala is an epithet of Indra, and it means, precisely, "he whose arm is his strength" — from kakuth, the shoulder-joint, the place where the arm meets the body. So the full verse is a three-fold identification in a fixed order:

  1. Manu — the ancestor-mind, the first man, the inherited pattern. Manas. The chest.
  2. Sūrya — the light, the eye, the thing seen and the capacity to be seen. Cakṣus. The eye.
  3. Kakuthsala / Indra — the arm, the shoulder, the capacity to act on what has been seen. Bala. The right arm.

Mind, eye, arm. Pattern, recognition, execution. A person who is unseen is not weak in all three; they are usually strong in the first and third and blocked in the second — they know who they are, they are capable of acting, and the middle term, the being-seen, does not fire. That is why the Āditya Hṛdayam does not fix this condition: it addresses the Sun as an object of praise, in the second person, and never installs the middle term at all.

The working form: Before any ritual, write the three clauses on a slip of paper, in your own handwriting, in this order — the pattern you inherited, the recognition you are missing, the action you would take if you had it. Vamadeva's sequence is not decorative. The tradition holds that you may not claim the second clause while lying about the first or the third, and the most common reason a Sun rite fails in an intelligent, capable native is that they are trying to purchase recognition without having named the inheritance they are still carrying.

3. The second hymn: the hawk who went up and came back down

There is a second Vamadeva hymn that the rite requires, and it is the one nobody teaches.

Ṛgveda 4.27 is spoken in the voice of the śyena — the hawk, the falcon — the bird that in the Vedic myth dives from the highest heaven and carries off the Soma while the archers shoot at it. The hymn's first person is the bird:

"I was the hawk… I brought the Soma from the highest sky."

This is the retrieval half of the doctrine, and its logic is exactly complementary to 4.26.

In 4.26 the movement is identity — I already am this. In 4.27 the movement is acquisition — something that is mine is being held elsewhere, at a height, guarded, and must be brought down. The Soma in this reading is not a drink. In remedial terms it is the thing that was taken from you and is currently being kept by someone else: your name, your credit, your share, your father's approval, the attribution for your own work.

Note the crucial detail of the hawk myth: the bird does not defeat the archers. It is struck — a feather is shot away — and it still arrives. The tradition preserves this because it is the honest structure of recognition. You do not get your name back intact and untouchable. You get it back damaged and in your possession. A native who is waiting to be recognised without having been wounded is waiting for a myth that does not exist, and this is a very large part of why the condition lasts thirty years.

The working form: Distinguish, in your own case, between the two operations and do not mix them. Identity work is done with the eyes closed, at sunrise, alone — it is an inner claim. Retrieval work is done with the eyes open, at the hour of the day when your particular loss occurred, and it is a claim made in the world — a letter sent, a credit demanded, a name re-attached to a thing you made. The tradition does not permit the second to be done only in meditation. The hawk flew.

4. The dog's intestines, and why this rishi is the one for people who feel disqualified

Now the part that the respectable tradition would prefer you did not know, and the part that makes this rite usable by the people who actually need it.

The dharma-texts record — and Vamadeva confesses it himself in the Ṛgveda — that during a famine he ate the flesh of a dog. The Manusmṛti and the Baudhāyana tradition both preserve the incident as the standard example of a Brahmin driven by hunger to the ultimate pollution, and both use him as a precedent for expiation rather than as a scandal. The Ṛgveda as oral-literature scholarship notes the same self-confession in his own hymns.

Sit with how strange this is.

The ṛṣi who declares "I became the Sun" is also the ṛṣi who admits, in writing, in the same collection, that he ate what a dog eats. The tradition did not suppress this. It kept it, and it kept it in the same man, and it did so deliberately, because the two facts are the same fact.

Here is the doctrinal point, and it is the reason this rite works where purity-based Sun remedies fail. Sūrya is the graha of purity, status, and the right to be seen in public. Every conventional Sun remedy therefore contains a hidden precondition: be clean first. Wear white. Bathe. Give up this. Be of good family. Do not eat that. And a native who already feels fundamentally disqualified — by birth, by divorce, by a criminal case, by poverty, by a body that does not pass, by the shame of a failure everyone knows about — will perform those remedies for years with a locked door in the middle of them. They are asking the Sun to recognise them while privately agreeing with the Sun's verdict against them.

Vamadeva's rite contains the pollution inside the ritual, as an ingredient. In the working form below, one element is a portion of food given to a dog — not thrown away as an offering to an inferior being, but fed, first, before the native eats. The rishi who ate the dog's food establishes the precedent that the dog's portion can precede the human's and the Sun does not withdraw. That single element is what makes the middle term — the being-seen — able to fire in a native who has been carrying a disqualification.

The working form: Do not attempt the mirror rite while holding an unconfessed disqualification. Name it once, in one sentence, out loud, to the sunrise, in the plainest words available. Not the legal version. The plain one. The tradition's own precedent is a man who said the worst thing about himself in a hymn that is still sung three thousand years later, and was not diminished by it.

5. The curse with a release clause

One more episode, from the Ganeśa Purāṇa, and it is the most practically useful thing in his biography.

A gandharva, flying past, accidentally trampled the sage. Vamadeva cursed him — and the curse turned him into a mouse. Then, when the gandharva begged, the sage did not simply withdraw the curse. He amended it: as a mouse, the gandharva would become the vāhana of Gaṇeśa — the mount of the remover of obstacles.

Look at the structure. He did not cancel the binding. He built a release into the binding, and the release was an upgrade.

This is the single most important piece of occult hygiene in the whole article, and it is the thing that separates a remedial rite from a slow-acting curse. When a native names the person or institution that denied them, and does it at a sunrise, with a mantra, in a formal frame — that is a curse, whether or not they intend it, whether or not they believe in curses. The Vedic tradition is entirely clear that a well-formed intention in a ritual frame does not evaporate. It installs.

And it installs against the person who spoke it as much as against the one named, because a curse with no release clause keeps the curser bound to the cursee — which is exactly the phenomenology of the native who cannot stop thinking about the person who wronged them. They are not obsessed. They are attached by their own un-released formula.

Vamadeva's solution is the model: never state a denial without stating its terminator in the same breath.

The working form: The formula is one sentence with two halves, spoken without a pause between them. "Until [the specific, observable condition], this stands; when that occurs, it is released and he is free of me and I of him." If you cannot name an observable terminating condition, you are not ready to speak the first half. This is not a moral instruction. It is a technical one: a clause without a terminator is a permanent installation, and permanent installations are what people come to astrologers to have removed.

6. The honest problem: the texts give the doctrine, not the procedure

Now the disclosure that every article in this series makes and that the popular ones never do.

The Ṛgveda gives Vamadeva's declaration. The Aitareya and Bṛhadāraṇyaka give the fact that he knew it in the womb. Neither gives a method. There is no Vamadeva-sūtra that says: take copper, take water, face east, do this twelve times.

What the tradition does give is (a) the verse with its exact three-fold structure, (b) the biographical precedents — the famine, the dog, the curse with a release clause, the hawk that was struck and arrived anyway, (c) the priestly context — he was a hotṛ-adjacent priest of a king performing an heir-rite, and (d) the lineage technique of the Aṅgirasas, which is the management of carried heat.

The rite in Part Three is assembled from those four things. It is a reconstruction, presented as such — the same way the Raikva rite was reconstructed from the saṃvarga doctrine, and the same way any working form in this tradition has always been reconstructed. What is not reconstruction is the verse, the order of its three clauses, the dog-portion precedent, or the release-clause principle. Those are in the sources.

Part Two: Why "Absorption" Is the Right Operation for an Unseen Chart

The four conditions people confuse with a weak Sun

Almost every "weak Sun" reading in the market collapses four different charts into one diagnosis, and then applies one remedy to all four, which is why the remedy so often does nothing.

Condition one: low vital heat. Cold hands, low stamina, weak digestion, no appetite for confrontation. Here the Sun is genuinely deficient in tejas. This responds to the conventional remedies — ruby, wheat, jaggery, Sūrya-namaskāra, Āditya Hṛdayam. If this is your chart, Vamadeva's rite is not the first thing you need. Heat first. The mirror rite presumes there is something to reflect.

Condition two: a blocked father-line. Sun conjunct Saturn, Sun in the sixth or eighth, Sun in the twelfth, Sun with Rāhu — and a family history in which the father was absent, humiliated, or himself unseen. Here the native's right to stand in public was never transmitted, because the transmitter was blocked. This is the single most common root of the "passed over" complaint and it is where the dog-portion element does its work.

Condition three: recognition withheld by an identifiable party. Not a chart condition at all in origin — a real, historical theft. A credit taken. A name removed from a paper. A family that decided, once, that this child was the ordinary one. The chart shows it as Sun-Rāhu or Sun in the eighth because a taken thing reads in a chart the way an eclipse reads in the sky. This is the condition the hawk hymn addresses, and it requires the retrieval operation, not the identity operation.

Condition four: the identity is intact and the middle term is not firing. This is Vamadeva's own condition — the man who knew what he was before he had a body to be it in. Strong first clause, strong third clause, blocked second. Capable, self-aware, and structurally invisible. This is the chart the rite below is built for, and it is the one condition in the list for which no standard remedy exists at all, because every standard remedy is a gift and the Sun does not take gifts.

The jyotiṣa reading of the condition

Look for these, in this order of weight:

  • Sun in the twelfth, and the native is not a renunciate. The twelfth is the house of the hidden; a Sun there gives a life lived backstage. If the native has genuinely renounced, this is a blessing. If they have not, it is the exact "unseen" signature.
  • Sun–Rāhu within nine degrees, or Sun in the same sign as Rāhu. This is a grahaṇa — an eclipse — and eclipses are structurally about the withdrawal of recognition, not the withdrawal of strength. An eclipsed Sun native is often extremely capable and consistently misattributed.
  • Sun in the sixth with a strong Mars. The capacity is enormous and the credit goes elsewhere; the chart is built to fight for other people's visibility.
  • Sun in the seventh, and the native's identity is entirely mediated by a partner. They are seen only as someone's spouse or someone's employee. Remove the relationship and the visibility goes to zero. This is a lending of the Sun, and it is repaid with interest at the wrong time.
  • Sun in Leo but in the eighth navāṃśa, or Sun vargottama in a hidden house. Strong in the rāśi, hidden in the subtle chart — the classic "he looks fine and is not seen" configuration.
  • The Ātma-kāraka is the Sun and the Sun is combust relative to nothing — i.e. the Sun itself is fine, and the dispositor is afflicted. The light is on and the wiring is cut downstream.

Notice what is not on this list: debilitation in Libra. A debilitated Sun in a good house with a strong dispositor produces a person who is often more visible than a strong one, because the Libran Sun learns to be seen through relationship and diplomacy. Debilitation is a style, not a sentence. The eclipse and the hidden house are the actual signatures of the condition this rite treats.

What "absorption" does that "strengthening" cannot

Every conventional Sun remedy is an addition from outside: a stone, a grain, a colour, a recitation. All of them operate on the model give the Sun something and it will give you something.

That model is structurally wrong for this graha, and the tradition knows it. The Sun is the Ātma-kāraka — the significator of the self. You cannot give the self a gift, because a gift implies two parties, and the Sun's entire function is to be the one party. Every dāna to the Sun is therefore a polite fiction: you are giving to a proxy, and the proxy only carries as far as your own conviction that you are the kind of person who deserves to be seen.

What Vamadeva's rite does instead is collapse the two parties. The operation is not strengthening but svarūpa-sthiti — establishment in one's own form. The three clauses are spoken in the first person, in the aorist, as a completed fact. The mirror is used because the reflection gives the eye a physical demonstration of the thing the verse is asserting: that there are two suns in the frame and only one of them sets.

That is the whole technique. Everything else in Part Three is scaffolding for that one perceptual event, repeated until it becomes the default.

Part Three: The Working Form — The Sūrya-Svarūpa Rite

Twelve days, beginning on a Sunday. Not a festival rite. A private installation.

What you need

  • A copper vessel — a small tumbler or lotā, unlined, not steel. Copper is the metal of the Sun and, in this rite, it is the vessel of the second sun.
  • A mirror — small, flat, and plain. Not a magnifying mirror, not a decorative one. A pocket mirror is correct. It must be a mirror you can lay flat and also hold at an angle.
  • Water — clean, in the copper vessel, filled fresh each morning. Not from a bottle that has sat in the sun.
  • Red flowers — twelve, one per day, if available; otherwise a single flower replaced daily, or red vermilion on the vessel's rim if no flower can be had. Do not substitute plastic.
  • A ghee lamp — one wick, lit only on the twelfth day and on any day you cannot see the sun.
  • Red thread — one length, long enough to tie around the copper vessel and then around your own right wrist.
  • A portion of food for a dog — decided in advance on day one, cooked or bought fresh on day twelve, and given before you eat anything that day. This is not optional, and it is the element that carries the rishi's authority. It is the reason the rite is available to people who feel unfit to perform it.
  • A slip of paper and a pen — for the three clauses.

Nothing here is expensive. The most important item is the mirror, and the most important item is not for sale in any temple shop.

When

Begin on a Sunday, in the twelve minutes after the upper rim of the sun clears the horizon. This is not a symbolic window. In the Vedic division of the day, the first portion after sunrise belongs to the Sun's vāma — his left, his beautiful, his un-struck aspect, before the heat begins and before the day's demands have been placed on the light. Begin inside that window or do not begin that day; the count does not restart, but the missed morning is added at the end.

Face east. Sit on the ground or on a mat, not on a chair, if your body permits. If it does not, sit however you can sit still for eleven minutes.

Twelve days from a Sunday ends on a Thursday — from Sun to Jupiter, from the father-significator to the lineage-blessing. That is deliberate: the rite opens the father-line and closes on the graha that can receive what was opened.

The rite, in order

1. Set the frame. Lay the mirror flat on the ground or on a low surface in front of you, facing up. Place the copper vessel of water on the mirror. The vessel now has two bottoms: copper, and the reflected sky. Light the lamp only if the sun is not visible.

2. Touch the three seats. Three touches, in Vamadeva's order, and this order is the whole transmission:

  • Right hand flat on the centre of the chest — the seat of Manu, of manas, of the inherited pattern.
  • Fingertips of the right hand to the right eye, closed, then the left eye — the seat of Sūrya, of cakṣus, of recognition.
  • Right hand gripping the right shoulder-joint — kakuth, the seat of Kakuthsala, of bala, of the arm that acts on what has been seen.

Do not rush this. Three touches, three breaths, one breath at each seat.

3. Speak the three clauses, in the first person, in the past completed tense. Use your own words if Sanskrit is not available to you, but keep the three-part structure and keep the tense:

aham manur abhavaṃ sūryaś cāham ahaṃ kakuth-salaḥ

"I was Manu. I became Sūrya. I am the arm that does."

Then, immediately, in your own language: "What I inherited, I have carried. What I became, I did not ask permission for. What I will do, I do with the arm and not with the waiting."

Say it once. Not eleven times, not a hundred and eight. Once. Vamadeva did not repeat himself; he reported.

4. The second sun. Now lift the copper vessel, still on the mirror, and tilt the mirror until the reflected sun falls into the water, and your own face appears in the water behind the reflection. Hold that arrangement for the length of twelve slow breaths. Do not close your eyes. The whole point of this step is that it is done with the eyes open, and the perceptual event — two suns, one of them mine — is the operative element. Everything else in the rite is preparation for these twelve breaths and the repetition of them.

If you cannot get the angle, place the mirror at roughly forty-five degrees against a wall and look down into the vessel. The geometry is forgiving. The eyes-open requirement is not.

5. Name the denial — with its terminator. On day one only, after step 4, state the specific denial in one sentence, and state its release in the same breath, without a pause:

"Until [the specific, observable condition], this stands. When that occurs, it is released, and he is free of me and I of him."

Then say: "The hawk was struck and arrived." Once.

If you have no specific denial — if the condition is diffuse, generational, nameless — skip the first half entirely and say only: "What was not given to my father was not given to me, and I take it now from the height and not from him." A curse aimed at a dead grandfather is a curse aimed at your own chest.

6. The flower. Place the red flower at the base of the copper vessel, on the mirror. Leave it. On the next morning, remove yesterday's flower and give it to flowing water or to a plant, never to the bin, and place the new one.

7. Close. Bow once. Do not blow out the lamp if one was lit; let it finish. Do not drink the water. Carry the vessel inside and set it down with the mirror beneath it — the arrangement stays assembled for all twelve days, in the east of the house, and nobody else handles it.

8. The dog's portion — day twelve only. Before you eat anything at all on the twelfth day, put the portion of food out for a dog and wait until it is taken, or leave it and do not look at it again. Say, once, while placing it: "The one who ate what a dog eats was still the sun." This is the disqualification clause. It is spoken before the final offering, not after.

9. The left-hand offering — day twelve only. On the final morning, at the same hour, perform step 4 again — the mirror, the water, the twelve breaths. Then, with the left hand, pour a thin stream of the water from the copper vessel onto the ground toward the east, and pour the remainder at the base of a tree, not down a drain.

The left hand is used only here, and only after eleven days of right-handed offering, and this sequence is not negotiable. The reason is doctrinal and it is the reason Vamadeva's name is what it is. The right-hand offering goes to the visible sun — the one everyone gives to, the one that rises and sets, the one the whole market addresses. The left-hand offering goes to vāma, the sun of the left, the inner sun, the light that does not rise and therefore cannot set. This is Vamadeva's own aspect: in the five-faced form of Śiva he is the vāma face, the beautiful northern face, the one that is turned away from the sacrifice and toward the interior.

Do not perform the left-hand offering first. A native who begins with the left hand has skipped the eleven days in which the visible sun teaches the eye what it is looking at, and the result is not illumination but a kind of inflated vagueness — the specific failure mode of every half-learned left-path practice.

10. Seal. Tie the red thread around the copper vessel, then cut it and tie the remaining length around your right wrist — the kakuth side. Keep it for forty days, then remove it and give it to flowing water. Turn the mirror face down and put it away. It is not used again for this purpose for one year.

The twelve days, in brief

  • Day 1 — full sequence, including the naming with its terminator.
  • Days 2–11 — steps 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7. No naming, no curse work, no additions. This is the flat part and it is where most people quit because nothing is happening.
  • Day 12 — the dog's portion before food, then steps 1–4, then the left-hand offering, then the seal.

What to expect, honestly

  • Days 1–3 commonly produce dreams of the father, or of a house you have not lived in for years. This is the first clause surfacing. It is not a sign of error and it is not a haunting. Do not interpret the dreams; note them and continue.
  • Days 4–7 often bring a person from the past back into contact — sometimes the exact person who withheld the recognition. This is the retrieval clause beginning to move. It is also the point at which natives most often abandon the rite, because the contact is unwelcome and they read it as the rite going wrong. It is not going wrong. The hawk is being shot at.
  • Days 8–11 are usually the quietest and the most important. The subjective sense is flatness. What is actually happening is that the twelve-breath event is becoming the default way the eye meets the morning, which is the entire mechanism.
  • Day 12 onward, the change is not dramatic and it is not in your feelings. It is in other people's behaviour — the meeting that gets scheduled, the credit that gets mentioned, the person who repeats your idea and this time says your name. This is why the rite cannot be self-assessed. Ask someone who knows you whether anything has changed in how you are treated, around day forty.
  • What it does not do. It does not produce fame, and it does not fix a genuinely weak Sun (Condition One). It does not return a stolen thing — it returns the attribution of it, which is a different object. And it does not work if the native continues to accept the original verdict privately. The mirror shows two suns; a man who believes he is the reflection will keep performing the rite forever and never once see it.

Contraindications

Do not perform this rite if:

  • The Sun is strong and unafflicted in the tenth, and the native is already visible. You will be installing a second sun into a sky that has one. The result in practice is grandiosity, and in a few cases a genuine break. The mirror rite amplifies whatever the native already believes about themselves; if that belief is already inflated, this is the wrong instrument.
  • There is an active psychiatric condition involving grandiosity or referentiality. This is the one rite in the series with a hard exclusion. The technique deliberately collapses the boundary between the self and a celestial object, which is safe in a stable native and not safe in an unstable one.
  • The father is dying or has just died. That is a different rite — the pitṛ work — and it must be done first and separately. The Vamadeva rite opens the father-line; it does not close a death.
  • The native is in acute material crisis. This takes twelve days and does not produce money. If there is no food this month, the Saṃvarga rite or a straightforward dāna is the correct instrument.
  • You intend to perform it for someone else without telling them. There is no proxy form. The verse is in the first person and it cannot be spoken by a stand-in — the entire mechanism is that the native's own eye meets the reflection. A mother performing this for her son's career will get, at best, her own visibility.

Closing

Vamadeva is remembered for a sentence he spoke before he had a life to speak it in.

He was not a king, not a lawgiver, not a founder of a school. He wrote one book of the Ṛgveda, cursed a king over some horses, cursed a gandharva into a mouse and then turned the mouse into a god's mount, ate what a dog eats during a famine and confessed it in writing, and declared — from inside his mother, before his eyes had opened — that he had already become the sun.

The tradition kept all of it. That is the point. It kept the declaration and the dog's flesh in the same man, and then it made that man the priest of a king's heir-rite, because a rishi who has been both things is the only kind who can be trusted with the question of who is allowed to be seen.

You do not become the sun by praising it. Praise keeps two parties in the room, and the whole difficulty of an unseen chart is that there are already two parties — the one you are, and the one the world has agreed to notice — and they are not the same person.

Vamadeva's technique is to make them the same person. Mirror, water, three seats, twelve breaths, once in the morning, twelve times. Then the small ordinary thing that follows: walking into a room and being counted.
