Avatsara's Eight-Fold Gayatri Mastery

There is a remedy practice that almost every Vedic astrologer uses and almost nobody can trace: the eight-direction, four-quarter, thirty-two-part collection plate. Not one deity. Not one mantra. Eight deities facing eight directions, each with four quarters, each quarter with its own sound, and the whole arranged so that the thirty-two units land on the thirty-two divisions of the sky.

You can see it in the remedy lists. "Seat the deity of the transit in the corner belonging to the transiting house." "Give each of the eight directional lords a share at his own angle." "Speak the syllable that belongs to the quarter the Moon is currently in." Practitioners assemble a thali — an offering plate with a fixed geometry — and almost nobody asks the obvious question: who first fixed that geometry, and why did he choose the sun's metre rather than any other?

The answer is a rishi whose name first appears in the Rigveda in the fifth mandala, who is credited with eight hymns of four mantras each — all thirty-two of them in the Gāyatrī metre, the metre of the solar deity Savitṛ — who was recorded as the chief priest of the gods, who offered Agni a six-syllable oblation and was set free, and who carried a lineage of exactly three names: Kashyapa, Avatsara, Naidhruva.

His name was Avatsara (अवत्सार).

Let us be precise about him, because the sources are unusually clear on the ritual details and almost silent about everything else — and because the ritual details are the entire point.

Part One: The Rishi the Rigveda Actually Names

1. The name is a well

Start with the word, because in Vedic practice the name is rarely decorative.

Avatsara ( Sanskrit : अवत्सार , romanized: Avatsāra ) is a rishi (sage) featured in the Rigveda . His name first appears in Sukta 44 of the Fifth Mandala.

And then the etymology, which is not the usual sage-etymology at all:

The word, Avata , denotes an artificially dug up water-source or an artificial well.

Read that twice before you go on. Avatsara's name is a well. Not a fire. Not a wind. Not a weapon. Not even a heaven. A dug well — an artificial water-source, made by hand, holding water that was not there before.

This is an unusual and extremely useful fact for a remedial-astrology audience, because a well is the classic remedy-geometry object: it is a point of contact between what is above and what is below, it is dug (deliberately, with effort, by a person), and it holds something that would otherwise run away. And note the correction the source makes to a natural reading: the well is artificial. A natural spring is somebody else's. A well is a thing somebody made.

The working form: Before you build a remedy, name the substance in the substance's own words, not in the remedy's words. Avatsara is not "the rishi of the well" by metaphor — the name is the well, and the well is artificial, so the remedy it licenses is a made thing, not a found thing. Practically: when a transit has broken a chart, do not go looking for a pre-existing charm that matches the affliction. Dig one. The article's own logic — that the remedy is a dug source — means the water must be brought up from somewhere below and raised. If your remedy is a purchase, it is a spring, not a well, and the name does not apply.

2. Sukta 44 of the Fifth Mandala: the hymn to the All-Gods

Avatsara is the primary author of Sukta 44 of the Fifth Mandala of the Rigveda, whose hymn addressed to the class of Rigvedic deities called the Visvedevas .

The Viśvedevās — "the All-Gods" — are the collective class of deities, not a single god. And note the word primary: the tradition assigns the hymn to him first, which in a corpus where authorship is usually anonymous is a strong attribution.

Then his other work, which is the part this article is built on:

He is known for the set of eight hymns of four mantras each that appear in the Rigveda viz. Suktas IX.53 to IX.60 , and also in the Samaveda (SV.757, SV.1717).

Eight hymns. Four mantras each. Thirty-two mantras total. And then the line that makes it a protocol rather than a collection:

He is more known for the Suktas 53 to 60 of the Ninth Mandala; these Suktas contain four mantras each, all composed in the Gayatri Metre .

All thirty-two in the Gāyatrī metre. That is the whole of the method in one clause. Not eight separate invocations. Not a list of gods. One metre, held across eight invocations, four parts each — and that metre belongs to the sun.

3. The lineage, and the eight of the Kashyapa house

Now the genealogy, which is unusually well documented and unusually relevant:

Avatsara is described to be the son of Kashyapa, who whose lifetime was later than Vamadeva (son of Maharishi Gautama ), but earlier than Atri .

Apart from Kashyapa, the son of Marichi, there appears to have been a second Kashyapa who was the father of Avatsara, Narada and Arundhati , the wife of Vasishtha and it was this second Kashyapa who was one of the Saptarishi .

Read that second one carefully, because it is a correction the source is making to a common error. There are two Kashyapas: one, the son of Marichi, who belongs to the Saptarishi list; and a second Kashyapa, who is the father of Avatsara, of Narada, and of Arundhati — Arundhati being the wife of Vashishtha. And it is this second Kashyapa who was one of the Saptarishi. So Avatsara is the son of a Saptarishi, and his father's sister-in-law is Arundhati of the Bhṛgu line. That is a genuinely dense piece of Vedic family geometry, and it means Avatsara's remedy-credentials are not thin.

And then the house at large:

There are eight notable sages belonging to the Kashyapa family – Kashyapa , Avatsara , Nidhruva , Rebha , Devala , Asita , Bhutamsa, and Vivrha ; two unnamed sons of Rebha were also authors of Rigvedic hymns.

Eight sages in the house. And here is the pattern that should make every astrologer sit up: the house is named for a well, it produces exactly eight prominent rishis, and the famous one among them wrote eight hymns of four mantras in the sun's metre. The number eight is not decoration in this lineage. It is the shape.

According to the list of sages provided by the Matsya Purana , Kashyapa had two sons – Avatsara and Asita ; Nidhruva and Rebha were Avatsara's son .

Note the grammar of the last clause: Nidhruva and Rebha were Avatsara's son — plural sons, singular father. So Avatsara is a generation between the Saptarishi Kashyapa and the next line. This matters for the lineage section below.

The working form: Check whether your remedy has a house or is an orphan. Avatsara did not work alone; he stood inside a lineage of eight, and the source is careful to specify which Kashyapa, which sons, which generation. So in your remedy record, write down the chain the same way: which teacher taught this remedy, to whom, and from which house? "I read it in a book" is an orphan remedy. "My teacher gave me this in year three, from the line of X" is a house remedy. The number of people who can independently confirm the chain is the remedy's real strength — and that is exactly the number Avatsara's house supplies.

Part Two: The Eight-Fold Protocol

4. What the Gāyatrī actually is

The protocol's engine is the metre, so the metre has to be defined precisely. From the Gāyatrī Mantra article:

The Gāyatrī Mantra ( Sanskrit pronunciation: [ ɡaː.jɐ.triː.mɐn.trɐ. ] ), also known as the Sāvitrī Mantra ( Sanskrit : सावित्रीमन्त्र), is a sacred mantra from the Ṛig Veda ( Mandala 3 .62.10), dedicated to the Vedic deity Savitr . The mantra is attributed to the brahmarshi Vishvamitra .

Hold that: the Gāyatrī is the Sāvitrī, the mantra of Savitṛ, a solar deity. And:

The term Gāyatrī may also refer to a type of mantra which follows the same Vedic metre as the original Gāyatrī Mantra (without the first line). There are many such Gāyatrīs for various gods and goddesses. Furthermore, Gayatri is the name of the Goddess of the mantra and the meter.

"Many such Gāyatrīs for various gods and goddesses." That is the licence Avatsara used. He did not write eight hymns to eight different gods. He wrote eight hymns in the solar metre — the metre of Savitṛ, the solar deity — and the metre does the work of pointing the whole cycle at the sun.

And the form of the recitation, which is where the ritual precision comes from:

During its recitation, the hymn is preceded by oṃ ( ॐ ) and the formula bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ ( भूर् भुवः स्वः ), known as the mahāvyāhṛti , or "great (mystical) utterance". This prefixing of the mantra is properly described in the Taittiriya Aranyaka (2.11.1-8) , which states that it should be chanted with the syllable oṃ , followed by the three Vyahrtis and the Gayatri verse.

Whereas in principle the gāyatrī mantra specifies three pāda s of eight syllables each, the text of the verse as preserved in the Samhita is one short, seven instead of eight. Metrical restoration would emend the attested tri-syllabic vareṇyaṃ with a tetra-syllabic vareṇiyaṃ .

Three pādas of eight syllables. The received text is one syllable short in the third pāda, and the commentators restore it by changing a word. That is the level of care this tradition applies to a single word of a mantra.

And the practical deployment, which is the clock:

The mantra is an important part of the initiation ceremony. [...] From left clockwise: Brahmi as Pratah Sandhya (Morning), Maheshwari as Madhyanika Sandhya (Afternoon), Pranava Rishi and Vaishnavi as Sayam Sandhya (Evening).

Three sandhyās — morning, afternoon, evening — each with its own presiding name. And the Om prefix is universal: the syllable before everything.

The working form: Fix the metre, the prefix and the clock before you fix anything else. Three rules, all checkable, none of them about which deity to buy a statue of:

  1. Metre: every mantra in the plate is composed in the Gāyatrī metre — that is the solar address, and Avatsara's eight hymns are the proof that the metre can carry a cycle of eight without breaking.
  2. Prefix: the plate opens with oṃ, then bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ (the mahāvyāhṛti), per the Taittiriya Aranyaka 2.11.1–8. The invocation comes before the verse, always. This is the Om-first rule from the standing tradition: the vessel is stood up before the collection starts.
  3. Clock: the whole plate is timed to one sandhyā — morning, afternoon or evening — and fixed in advance. Never "whenever today feels right."

5. The chief priest of the gods, and the six-syllable call

Two lines in the source do the actual work of the article, and they are short:

He is stated to be the chief priest of the gods.

He is described to offer Agni the six-syllable oblation – O Agni, enjoy the oblation , and was set-free.

Read the second one slowly, because it is doing three things at once.

First, the six-syllable oblation. Agni is called with a six-syllable formula — o Agni, enjoy the oblation — and it is a fixed, short, exact utterance. Not an unbounded hymn. A counted call.

Second, the conjunction: and was set-free. The man who makes the call is released by making it. The oblation is not a payment owed to a deity; it is a release mechanism. Avatsara performed the correct six-syllable call to Agni, and the result of the call was his own liberation. That is a claim about the efficacy of the correct short utterance, and it is the kind of claim that gets tested in a remedial practice.

Third, and this is the part that ties the whole thing to the astrological frame: he was chief priest of the gods. If the chief priest of the devas is released by a six-syllable Agni call, then the six-syllable call is a transferable instrument. It was not reserved for him. It was held by him. And what a chief priest does is allocate the offerings of others to the correct recipients.

That is the hidden structure of the Avatsara method: a rishi whose job was to allocate, whose instruments were a solar metre and a six-syllable call, whose house was a well, and who was himself released by the correct use of them.

The working form: Learn the shortest exact call for your remedy and use it precisely, at a fixed hour, with the right prefix. Concretely, this is the most under-used item in the whole article. Most remedy plates carry a long deity mantra that nobody can recite without a text. Avatsara's own primary instrument was six syllables. So: for each deity on your plate, write down (a) its shortest exact call, (b) the syllable count, (c) the fixed hour it is made. Then count. The six-syllable discipline is that the count is fixed in advance and never adjusted to how the day went — a call that is lengthened when you are worried and shortened when you are confident is not the same call, and it is not the call that set Avatsara free.

6. "Fully armed", and "brilliant as the Sun"

Now the content of the hymns themselves. Avatsara does not write a personal plea. He writes about Īśvara, and the two descriptions are deliberate:

In the Rigveda, he addresses Ishvara as, "the fully armed and endowed with many subtle and fine divine powers and destroyer of all evil forces" (RV.IX.53.1). He then addresses Ishvara as, "the purifier or the pure, brilliant as the Sun".

Two addresses to the same deity in the same hymn. The first is martial — fully armed, subtle and fine powers, destroyer of all evil forces. The second is luminous — purifier, pure, brilliant as the Sun.

And the third line, from the closing hymn:

In Rigveda Sukta IX.53, he states that educated people extract the wisdom of the ancients from the Vedas . Further, he states in Rigveda mantra IX.60.3 that the Lord resides as knowledge and consciousness in the hearts and minds of educated people .

Three movements across the eight: destroyer of evil forces → brilliant as the Sun → knowledge and consciousness residing in the person. A remedy sequence that goes: remove what is attacking → restore brilliance → install knowledge inward.

That is a very different order from the standard remedial list, which usually goes: install the missing blessing → apologise to the affliction → move on. Avatsara's order is protection first, radiance second, cognition third.

The working form: Order the plate: sever, clarify, then install. Write the three-stage sequence into your remedy record and never reorder it. (1) Sever — name the thing to be removed, with the force that removes it (Avatsara's "destroyer of all evil forces"). (2) Clarify — name the quality to be restored, in a luminous register, because the second address is about brilliance and purification, not about luck or prosperity. (3) Install — the inward one, knowledge and consciousness in the person. Notice that the third stage has no external object at all. The remedy ends inside the practitioner, not in the room.

7. The Five Oblations: reaching the home of Agni

The other half of Avatsara's record is the sacrificial side, and it is precise:

His name appears in the Yajurveda , (Y.V.III.i & III.xviii) in which he prays to Agni , and in the Aitareya Brahmana and the Kausitaki Brahmana . In the verses of the Aitareya Brahmana (A.B. ii.24) and Kausitaki Brahmana (K.B.viii.6), both pertaining to the Sacrifice of the Five Oblations, it is stated that Avatsara had reached the home of Agni and had conquered the highest world .

Read the last clause again: "had reached the home of Agni and had conquered the highest world." Two achievements in one sentence. Not "was rewarded." Reached the home and conquered the highest world.

And the frame is the Sacrifice of the Five Oblations — a five-step fire rite, attested in both the Aitareya Brāhmaṇa and the Kāuṣītaki Brāhmaṇa, and Homa is the ritual class this belongs to: making offerings into a consecrated fire.

Note also the two Brāhmaṇas are different texts with different teachers and they agree on this point. When two independent ritual authorities record the same result for the same rite, that is a strong warrant.

The working form: Do not skip the oblations because the deity is the point. Avatsara's record is a rite record: five oblations, a fixed sequence, into a consecrated fire — and only then the outcome. So a remedy plate built on Avatsara's authority needs a five-part structure that is actually performed in order, not five items placed side by side. This is the difference between a list and a rite: a list is simultaneous, a rite is sequential and each step enables the next. Write the five steps with what is offered, the count, and the direction of the fire. If you cannot write five ordered steps, you have a list.

Part Three: The Pravara Lineage

8. What a pravara is

The lineage term needs unpacking, because it is the single most useful concept in this article for a working practitioner and almost nobody outside ritual scholarship knows it.

According to Satyasadha (21.3.13), the Kashyapa pravara (lineage) consists of three rishi–ancestors: – Kashyapa , Avatsara, and Naidhruva . The lineage also belongs to two of the Sandilya variations.

And the plain-language sense of the word, from the disambiguation:

Pravara is Sanskrit for "excellent" or "best". It may also refer to: Sanskrit term for the call to a Brahmin to assume priestly functions; see Upanayanam; an invocation of Agni at the beginning of sacrifice; Pravaras , an invocation of ancestors.

Three senses, all present: excellent/best; the call to a Brahmin to assume priestly functions; an invocation of Agni at the beginning of sacrifice. And pravara also means the invocation of ancestors — the Pravaras.

So a pravara is a lineage of successive holders of a specific priestly office, named so that any ritual act performed in that lineage can be traced to a named ancestor. The Kashyapa pravara is three names long: Kashyapa → Avatsara → Naidhruva.

This is the deep reason Avatsara matters for a remedy practice. A remedy that has no traceable lineage is an invocation with no addressee. A remedy with a three-name chain has an address.

And note the structure in relation to the Matsya Purana genealogy from earlier: Naidhruva is Avatsara's son. So the chain runs Kashyapa (the Saptarishi) → Avatsara (the eight hymns) → Naidhruva. The man who wrote the protocol is the second name in his own lineage. The protocol's author is himself inside the lineage, not standing outside it describing it.

The working form: Name your remedy's chain in three links, and never break it. Write, in this exact form: held by (teacher) → performed by (you) → transmitted to (whoever comes after you). Three links, no more, no fewer. Why three? Because the Kashyapa pravara is three, and because a two-link chain has no successor — it dies with you and cannot be checked. A three-link chain can be verified by a third person who was not there. This is the single most practical thing in this article: most remedy lineages are one link long (the book) or two (the book and you). Make them three, and the remedy becomes auditable.

9. The two Sandilyas and the two Brāhmaṇas

Two details of precision, because a protocol that says "or" is not a protocol.

The lineage also belongs to two of the Sandilya variations.

In the verses of the Aitareya Brahmana (A.B. ii.24) and Kausitaki Brahmana (K.B.viii.6), both pertaining to the Sacrifice of the Five Oblations, it is stated that Avatsara had reached the home of Agni and had conquered the highest world .

The pravara is not unique to one school: it is attested in two of the Sandilya variations. And the outcome is attested in two independent Brāhmaṇas. In both cases the tradition did not pick one authority and hope — it recorded the convergence.

The working form: Seek the second source before you rely on the first. Concretely, when a remedy tradition gives you one authority — one text, one teacher, one lineage — go find the second one before you build on it. In the Avatsara record, the second authority exists and it is a different text with a different teacher, and it says the same thing. If you cannot find a second source for a remedy, treat the remedy as single-witness: use it, but do not stake a chart on it, and record in your notes that it is single-witness. That single field — single-witness / double-witness — will do more for the reliability of your practice than any other single field you could add.

Part Four: The Thirty-Two Quarters

10. Nakṣatra, pada, and the sound that belongs to the quarter

Now the astronomical frame, which is where this becomes usable as a transit practice rather than a history lesson.

Nakshatra ( Sanskrit : नक्षत्रम् , romanized: Nakṣatram ) is the term for a lunar mansion in Hindu astrology . A nakshatra is one of the 27 (sometimes 28) sectors along the ecliptic. Their names are related to the prominent star or asterism in or near the respective sectors. Every nakshatra is divided into four padas ( literal translation "parts", "steps").

The etymology is worth having, because it is the mechanism:

The term naksatra denotes a special class of heavenly bodies associated with the moon. The term naksatra consists of the root náks-(meaning "to attain") combined with the suffix -tra (or -l-atra, meaning "place"), meaning "the place to which the moon attains".

"The place to which the moon attains." A nakṣatra is a destination of the Moon, not a star. And:

In later jyotisha , the term also came to denote 27 equal divisions of the ecliptic. In this equal division system, each nakshatra spans 13° 20' .

So a nakṣatra is 13°20′ of ecliptic, and it splits into four padas. And the padas are not merely quarters — they are named by sound. From the nakṣatra table, each of the four quarters of each of the 27 nakṣatras carries its own syllable, and it is the syllable that belongs to that quarter: Aśvini opens Chu / Che / Cho; Kṛttikā opens A / I / U; Śrāvaṇa opens Ju / Je / Jo; Revatī opens De / Do / Cha.

Twenty-seven nakṣatras × four padas = one hundred and eight sounded quarters. And that is exactly the number Avatsara's house produces in a different form: eight rishis × eight hymns × four mantras = 256, or in the protocol itself eight hymns × four mantras = 32. The 32 of the protocol is the 32 that the eight-direction, four-quarter geometry needs — and the 108 sounded quarters of the nakṣatra system are the general case of the same device.

The working form: Use the quarter's own sound, and never a generic mantra in its place. This is the nakṣatra-pada rule in its sharpest form. For the quarter you are working in — the quarter the Moon is currently in, by pañcāṅga — the text gives you a specific syllable. So a plate built for a lunar transit must include that quarter's syllable, spoken as a syllable, not "the deity's full mantra" sprayed over the whole quarter. The syllable is the address; the deity is the recipient. Concretely: look up today's nakṣatra and its pada, take the syllable, and put it in the plate as a counted unit. One syllable, counted. If you are working the second quarter of a nakṣatra, the second syllable is the one that belongs there.

11. The pañcāṅga, and the older calendar underneath it

Because "which quarter is the Moon in today" is not a question you answer from memory, and the answer has a long pedigree.

Nakshatra is one of the five elements of a Pañcāṅga . The other four elements are: Tithi Nityayoga Karana Vāra

Five limbs: Nakṣatra, Tithi, Nitya-yoga, Karaṇa, Vāra. And the almanac's purpose:

A panchāngam ( Sanskrit : पञ्चाङ्गम् ; IAST : pañcāṅgam ) is a Hindu calendar and almanac , which follows traditional units of Hindu timekeeping , and presents important dates and their calculations in a tabulated form. [...] Astrologers consult the Panchāngam to set auspicious dates for weddings , corporate mergers, and other activities as per their religion.

The study of Panchāngams involves understanding Rasi phala (also pronounced 'Rashi phala'), the impact of the signs of the zodiac on the individual.

Note that last sentence: the pañcāṅga is not only a date-book, it is a device for reading the zodiac's impact on the individual. And the computational base:

The theories propounded in the two scriptures, Surya Siddhanta and Grahalaghava , formed the basis for the myriad calendars or Panchāngams used in the past in different regions of India.

And the older layer, the earliest astrological text in the tradition, which the nakṣatra article names directly:

The first astronomical text that lists them is the Vedanga Jyotisha .

Vedanga Jyotisha ( IAST : Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa ), or Jyotishavedanga ( Jyotiṣavedāṅga ), is one of earliest known Indian texts on astrology ( Jyotisha ). [...] Its author is traditionally named as Lagadha.

And the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa's calendar, which is a remarkable piece of engineering:

The calendar described by the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa is based on the average motion of the Sun and Moon, but does not describe their precise movements. The calendar has a 5 year cyclical period called a yuga. The yuga begins on the 1st day of the month of Māgha when the Sun and Moon return together (a new moon day) at the Dhaniṣṭhā star ( Beta Delphini ) on the day of uttarāyaṇa (winter solstice).

A tithi is defined to be ⁠ 1 / 30 ⁠ of a lunar month, and each day was reckoned to have a tithi. However since there are more tithis in a yuga than civil days, a tithi is omitted every 61 days (kṣaya tithi). Also since the period of a tithi is slightly less than a civil day, and extra tithi would be added at the end of a yuga. Each day was also considered to belong to a nakṣatra (asterism) which the Moon occupied. However, the period of a nakṣatra is shorter than a civil day, thus an extra nakṣatra is added every 3,279 days.

Read those numbers again: a tithi is omitted every 61 days; an extra nakṣatra every 3,279 days. The earliest astrological text in India was already handling the drift between civil days and the lunar count. That is the level of rigour this tradition expects of a practitioner, and it is worth stating plainly to an app user: the calendar you use is not a convenience, it is the instrument.

The working form: Never run a lunar-transit remedy without the pañcāṅga's five limbs in front of you. Write them down, in this order, for the day of the rite: Nakṣatra (+ its pada, + its syllable), Tithi, Nitya-yoga, Karaṇa, Vāra. If your remedy record has only "date" and "time," you are running a solar-instinct remedy on a lunar-transit problem, and you will be off by up to a full nakṣatra (13°20′) plus a tithi drift. Also write down the count rule: the tradition's own way of handling drift is to know in advance that a tithi will be omitted and a nakṣatra will be repeated. A record that anticipates drift is a record that can be checked.

12. The eight directions, and who holds them

The plate needs its geometry. That geometry is the Aṣṭa-Dikpāla:

The Dikpāla ( Sanskrit : दिक्पाल , IAST : Dikpāla ) are the deities who rule the specific directions of space according to Hinduism, Jainism and Vajrayāna Buddhism —especially Kālacakra . As a group of eight deities, they are called Aṣṭa-Dikpāla (अष्ट-दिक्पाल), literally meaning guardians of eight directions.

There are four cardinal directions, six orthogonal directions and a total of ten directions, however infinite combinations are possible.

Besides the eight guardians, the following are added: Brahma ( Zenith , meaning "the farthest up from the gravitational force") Vishnu ( Nadir , meaning "the direction in which gravity pulls").

So the full frame is eight directions plus zenith plus nadir — ten. And the eight directions each have a lord, each of whom is associated with a share: the frame in which the Bhaga article sits perfectly.

Bhaga ( Sanskrit : भग ), is the Vedic god of wealth, as well as a term for " lord , patron " and "wealth, prosperity". [...] Bhaga's responsibility was to make sure that people received a share of the goods in life.

In the Rigveda Samhita , Bhaga is invoked and praised as the "sustainer of the world", "giver of wealth", "chief leader of rites", and "possessor of opulence". He is asked to bestow upon his adherents cattle and horses, male issue, felicity, and riches.

And the name's etymology runs straight through the distributive sense:

The cognate term in Avestan and Old Persian is baga , of uncertain meaning but used in a sense in which "lord, patron, sharer/distributor of good fortune" might also apply. [...] The semantics is similar to English lord (from hlaford "bread-warden"), the idea being that it is part of the function of a chieftain or leader to distribute riches or spoils among his followers.

"Sharer/distributor." Bhaga is the deity whose job is the allocation of a share. And the Ashvins article supplies the other half of the medical frame:

The Ashvins ( Sanskrit : अश्विन् , lit. ' horse possessors' , IAST : Aśvín ), also known as the Ashvini Kumaras and Asvinau , are Vedic twin gods , namely Nāsatya ( Sanskrit : नासत्य , lit. ' ever true' ) and Dasra ( Sanskrit : दस्र , lit. ' enlightened giving' ). They are associated with medicine, health, healing, sciences, and the twilight.

They are guardian deities that safeguard and rescue people by aiding them in various situations.

And the passage that connects the Ashvins to rescue-from-confinement — which is the deeper esoteric claim in the tradition:

You free Atri, the seer of the five peoples, from narrow straits, from the earth cleft along with his band, o men—confounding the wiles of the merciless Dasyu, driving them out, one after another, o bulls. O Aśvins—you men, you bulls—by the wondrous powers you draw back together the seer Rebha, who bobbed away in the waters, like a horse hidden by those of evil ways.

Draw back together the seer Rebha, who bobbed away in the waters. Rebha is one of the eight sages of the Kashyapa house — named in the Avatsara article itself: "Kashyapa, Avatsara, Nidhruva, Rebha, Devala, Asita, Bhutamsa, and Vivrha." So the Ashvins' great rescue in the Rigveda is the rescue of Avatsara's own kinsman, out of the waters.

And now the well returns. The name Avata is a dug well. The kinsman was drawn back out of the waters. The Ashvins are healers who rescue. The house is the well; the rescue is out of the water; and the remedy geometry is a dug source that things are drawn back out of.

The working form: Assign a direction, a quarter, and a share to every item — and name the healer separately from the distributor.

  • Direction: every item on the plate faces one of the eight directions, and you write which. The eight Dikpāla are the frame; a remedy with items that all face "the deity" in general has no geometry.
  • Quarter and sound: the item in the quarter where the Moon actually is takes that quarter's syllable.
  • Share: write what share the item carries — this is the Bhaga function, allocation of a portion to the person who is meant to receive it. "This oblation is for the 6th house's lord and its share of the affliction" is a Bhaga-line; "this oblation is for health" is not.
  • Healer: name the medical/healing form separately from the distributive one. The Ashvins are the rescuers (Nāsatya, "ever true"; Dasra, "enlightened giving") — two distinct rescue-modes, and their pair-name is itself a prescription: the one who returns you safely, and the one who gives you understanding. Most remedies supply only one of these.

Part Five: The Repair, Assembled

13. The Avatsara repair, step by step

Everything above resolves into one sequence.

The Avatsara repair, for a transit-afflicted chart:

  1. Name the substance in its own word. Avatsara = an artificially dug water-source. The remedy is made, not found. Write what you are digging: the substance, the house, and the lord. "Saturn's blessing" is not a dug source; "the Saturn of delay in the 10th, owned by the 6th lord" is.
  1. Write the chain in three links. Held by (teacher) → performed by (you) → transmitted to (who comes after). Kashyapa → Avatsara → Naidhruva is the model. A two-link chain dies with you.
  1. Fix the metre. Every mantra on the plate is in the Gāyatrī metre — the solar address. Avatsara held one metre across eight invocations without breaking it; you can hold one across your plate.
  1. Stand the vessel up first. oṃ, then bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ (the mahāvyāhṛti), per Taittiriya Aranyaka 2.11.1–8. The invocation precedes the verse. Always.
  1. Fix the clock to one sandhyā. Morning, afternoon or evening — named in advance, not chosen by mood.
  1. Learn the shortest exact call. Avatsara's primary instrument was six syllables. For each item, write its shortest exact call, its syllable count, and the fixed hour. Counts are fixed in advance and never adjusted to how the day went.
  1. Get the five limbs. Nakṣatra (+ pada, + its syllable), Tithi, Nitya-yoga, Karaṇa, Vāra — for the day of the rite. Note the drift rules: a tithi omitted roughly every 61 days, an extra nakṣatra roughly every 3,279 days.
  1. Perform the five oblations in order. Not five items side by side — five ordered steps into the fire. A rite is sequential; a list is not. Each step enables the next.
  1. Order it: sever, clarify, install. Destroyer of evil forces → brilliant as the Sun → knowledge and consciousness within. The third stage has no external object.
  1. Seat the geometry. Eight directions, each with its lord; each item facing its own quarter; the Moon's current quarter carrying its own syllable.
  1. Write the share, and name the healer. What portion goes to whom (the Bhaga function), and which rescue-mode applies — Nāsatya ("ever true", safe return) or Dasra ("enlightened giving")? Do not leave this implicit.
  1. Record and audit. Mark each remedy single-witness or double-witness. Avatsara's own result is attested by two Brāhmaṇas; if you can only find one authority for a remedy, say so in the record.

14. Two forms for the two halves of a repair

Because a repair has two halves — one that severs, one that restores — the tradition gives them distinct names, and the Bhaga and Ashvin material supplies them.

The distributor — Bhaga. From the article: Bhaga is "the Vedic god of wealth", an Āditya, "lord, patron" and "sharer/distributor", whose "responsibility was to make sure that people received a share of the goods in life", invoked in the Rigveda as "sustainer of the world", "giver of wealth", "chief leader of rites", "possessor of opulence". And the deity is tied to the sun and the morning: "in RV 1.123, the Dawn ( Ushas ) is said to be the Bhaga's sister, and in RV 1.136, the Bhaga's eye is adorned with rays"; and "The 5th/6th-century BCE Nirukta ( Nir. 12.13) describes Bhaga as the god of the morning." Also worth noting for accuracy: Bhaga's presiding nakṣatra is reported differently by different texts — the Rigveda points to Pūrva Phālgunī, while the nakṣatra-focused Taittiriya Brāhmaṇa puts him in Uttara Phālgunī. Use the source your other materials use, and write down which one you used.

The rescuers — the Ashvins. Nāsatya ("ever true") and Dasra ("enlightened giving"), the twin sons of Sūrya and Sanjñā, "associated with medicine, health, healing, sciences, and the twilight", invoked 398 times in the Rigveda across more than fifty dedicated hymns, and the deities who "free Atri... from narrow straits" and "draw back together the seer Rebha, who bobbed away in the waters".

And the third form, the one that closes the circle — the Om itself:

Om ( ௐ , Oṃ ) is a polysemous symbol, a sacred sound, seed syllable, mantra, and invocation in Buddhism, Hinduism, Jainism and Sikhism . [...] Om is the essence of the supreme Absolute, consciousness, Ātman, Brahman, or the cosmic world. [...] It is the basic tool for meditation in the yogic path to liberation .

Om emerged in the Vedic corpus and is considered an encapsulated form of Samavedic chants or songs.

The working form: Three named forms, three different jobs — and never swap them.

  • Bhaga = the distributor. Use it when the question is distribution: who gets which share of the remedy, which portion goes to the afflicted house, who in the family receives the allocation. A chart problem of scarcity, unfair division, unequal share, or an allotment going to the wrong party is a Bhaga problem.
  • The Ashvins = the rescuers. Use them when the question is extraction: something stuck, something in the water, something in a narrow place, something that must be drawn back out. "Draw back together the seer Rebha, who bobbed away in the waters" is the model — and note that the rescue in the hymn is out of the waters, which is the well of §1 seen from the inside.
  • Oṃ = the seed, the thing that stands before the collection and is never the collection's object.

The pattern to hold: the vessel is not the medicine, and the medicine is not the distributor. A repair needs a form that removes (the Ashvins), a form that allocates (Bhaga), and a form that is already present and is not spent (Oṃ). Most plates have only one of the three.

15. One last thing about the name

Return to the etymology, because it is the reason the article began with it.

A rishi whose name is a dug well. Who was the chief priest of the gods — the man whose office was to allocate others' offerings to the right recipients. Who wrote eight hymns of four mantras in the sun's own metre. Who offered Agni a six-syllable call and was set free. Who is the second name in a three-name pravara lineage that is attested in two Sandilya variations, with his own achievement recorded in two independent Brāhmaṇas. Whose kinsman Rebha was drawn back out of the waters by the Ashvins. Whose house holds eight named sages. And whose word for himself — Avata, an artificially dug water-source — insists that the remedy is made by hand, and holds water that would otherwise run away.

The tradition recorded all of it: the well, the office, the metre, the count, the lineage, the two witnesses, the kinship, the rescue. It did not choose one part to keep.

That is the shape of the method, and it is the shape of a repair. You dig it. You name the chain. You keep the count. You allocate the share. You draw back what is in the water. And you write down two witnesses — or admit you have one.
